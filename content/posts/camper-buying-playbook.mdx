Every source worth trusting says the same thing: roof, seams, windows, and slide seals first, before you let the interior win you over. Press the walls and ceilings gently in the corners and around the vents, and sight down the sidewalls for bulges where the fiberglass has separated from the plywood behind it. That separation is called delamination, and [RVTrader's inspection guide](https://www.rvtrader.com/blog/2025/06/11/inspecting-a-used-rv-always-check-these-10-things/) names water the number-one enemy of a used RV. Bring a cheap moisture meter; it catches the damage paint hides. Check the tire date codes, too. Tires die of old age, not mileage, and a 33-footer takes four to six of them.

Slide-outs deserve a second thought. They're why a 31-footer feels like a home instead of a vehicle, and parked full-time they can stay out for years without trouble. But they're the worst-insulated part of the rig and the most likely place to leak — which matters in a cold winter with a family breathing inside. A dehumidifier and real ventilation discipline aren't optional when people are living in it; [Camp Addict](https://campaddict.com/rv-slide-outs/) is blunt about those joints leaking air. Run every slide fully in and out during the visit and make sure it seals tight when closed.

Then there's the truck math, which most buyers do backwards. A 33-foot bunkhouse lands around 9,000 to 10,000 pounds loaded, with roughly a thousand pounds of tongue weight, and a typical half-ton pickup carries 1,200 to 1,800 pounds of payload. A family plus gear plus that tongue weight eats nearly all of it. [The owner consensus](https://www.rvlifemag.com/towing-half-ton-three-quarter-ton/) lands on a gas 3/4-ton, F-250 or Silverado 2500 class, as the comfortable pick; no diesel needed. So when comparing campers, write down each unit's GVWR — that number decides the truck you buy later.

And when buying from a private seller: never send a deposit before standing in front of it, get the VIN in advance and run it, and match the VIN on the trailer to the title and the seller's ID.

Three photos to take on the first visit: roof seams, tire date codes, and the slide seals. They'll tell you more than the listing ever will.
